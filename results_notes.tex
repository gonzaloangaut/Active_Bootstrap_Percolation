	\section{\label{sec:level1}Model}
	
	We consider a system composed of two cell phenotypes: round (passive) and elongated (motile). The latter can exhibit variable aspect ratios, constrained within the range $\phi$, with $1<\phi<\phi_{max}=2.7$. A novel aspect of our model is the plasticity of cell phenotype: round cells can elongate, and elongated cells can contract. Furthermore, the intrinsic speed of a cell increases linearly with the aspect ratio, vanishing for round cells and reaching unity for those with maximum aspect ratio.
	
	The basic idea of elongation is the tendency of cells to deform and migrate when local space is available. On the other hand, elongated cells shrink if they experience enough force. To implement these ideas to our model, we apply the following mechanisms:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A round cell attempts to elongate by sampling a random angle $\theta \in [0, 2 \pi) $. For this direction, we calculate the overlap of the potentially deformed cell with its neighbors. If the overlap is below a defined threshold for all neighbors, the angle becomes a candidate. We repeat this procedure for 10 trials. Finally, we select the candidate direction that minimizes the total overlap. If no candidate satisfies the threshold condition, the cell remains round.
		\item Elongated cells exhibit two behaviors. They can shrink if the force exerted by neighbors is sufficient to arrest motion in the direction of the intrinsic velocity. If this does not happen, they can try to continue their elongation in the direction of motion.
	\end{itemize}
	

	For the initial conditions, the system starts with all cells in the round state, placed randomly. We wait some time for the system to relax and avoid large overlaps before the elongation dynamics begin. 
	
	\section{\label{sec:level2} Classification of States}
	
	
	Figure \ref{fig:examples} shows examples of the steady state for different system densities. At low densities, the system is dominated by elongated cells, which can be either isolated or form small clusters. As the density increases, the fraction of elongated cells decreases, and they are more likely to form clusters rather than move in isolation, while the population of round cells grows, ultimately reaching a state where the entire system is composed of round cells.

	Broadly speaking, two different states can be identified. The first one, where cells find space to elongate (as at low densities in Fig. \ref{fig:examples}), which we associate with an unjammed state, and a second one where all cells remain round, forming a jammed state (as in $\rho = 0.9$ in Fig. \ref{fig:examples}).
	
	In order to classify each realization as jammed or unjammed, both the largest cluster size of round cells ($S_{max}$) and the fraction of active cells ($f_{a}$) are analyzed. It is expected that unjammed states have a significant fraction of elongated cells, while jammed states are characterized by a giant cluster of round cells. Fig. \ref{fig:classification} (a) shows a scatter plot of $S_{max}/N$ vs $f_{a}$, and (b) the distribution of $f_{a}$. From this classification, we compute the jamming probability as the fraction of realizations classified as jammed for each density, shown in Fig. \ref{fig:classification} (d). A clear transition between unjammed and jammed states can be observed as the density increases: the system evolves from predominantly unjammed configurations at low densities to fully jammed states at high densities. The relatively sharp change in the jamming probability indicates a well-defined transition between these two regimes.

	\section{\label{sec:level3} Spatial Organization and Structure of Clusters}
	
	
	To characterize the spatial organization of the system, we analyze the cluster structure formed by each type of cells. In order to do so, we define that two cells belong to the same cluster if they fulfill two conditions: (i) They are of the same type (both elongated or both round), and (ii) they are close enough to interact, allowing for a small tolerance $\delta$, such that cells that are within a distance slightly larger than the interaction threshold are still considered connected.
	
	Figure \ref{fig:cluster_metrics} presents the evolution of several cluster observables as a function of density for both round and elongated cells. Panel (a) shows the normalized size of the largest cluster, $\frac{S_1}{N}$. At low densities, elongated cells form extended but fragmented structures, with the largest cluster already reaching a significant fraction of the system. As density increases, these structures progressively merge, and $\frac{S_1}{N}$ reaches a maximum around $\rho \approx 0.6$, indicating the emergence of a dominant elongated stream. At higher densities, however, the size of the largest elongated cluster decreases, eventually vanishing as the system becomes jammed.
	
	In contrast, round cells exhibit a percolation-like transition: the largest cluster grows continuously at first and then undergoes an abrupt increase at high densities, eventually spanning the entire system.

	Panels (b) and (c) provide further insight into the evolution of cluster competition. For round cells, the second largest cluster exhibits a noticeable peak, reaching approximately $6\%$ of the system size just before the transition. This indicates an increase in large-scale fluctuations and the presence of sizable secondary structures near the critical region. However, the ratio 
	$S_2/S_1$ decreases in this same density range, showing that the largest cluster grows even more rapidly and progressively dominates the system as the transition is approached.
	
	For elongated cells, the ratio $S_2/S_1$ is relatively large at low densities ($\approx 0.3$), consistent with a fragmented, mosaic-like organization in which several streams coexist. As density increases, this ratio decreases and reaches a minimum in the range $\rho \in [0.6, 0.76]$, signaling the consolidation of a single dominant elongated cluster that governs the collective migration.
	
	Finally, panel (d) shows the mean number of clusters. At low densities, elongated cells form a large number of small clusters, reflecting a highly fragmented active phase. As density increases, the number of clusters decreases while $S_1$ increases, indicating progressive coalescence into fewer and larger structures. This behavior is consistent with the formation of a coherent oncostream at intermediate densities. Near the jamming transition, the number of elongated clusters drops sharply due to the progressive loss of activity. 
	
	In contrast, round cells maintain a relatively constant number of clusters, between $100$ and $200$ (corresponding to $1\%$–$2\%$ of the system size), before collapsing to zero at the transition point.

	
	Overall, these results reveal distinct structural regimes in the system. For elongated cells, three main behaviors can be identified. At low densities, the active phase is characterized by a fragmented, mosaic-like organization, with many independent clusters and no clearly dominant structure. As density increases, these clusters progressively merge, and in the range $\rho \approx 0.6–0.7$ a single dominant elongated stream emerges, governing the collective migration. As the system approaches the jamming transition, activity progressively decreases and the elongated structures lose cohesion.
	
	For round cells, the system remains spatially heterogeneous over a broad density range, with several finite clusters coexisting. As density increases further, the largest round-cell cluster grows rapidly and eventually dominates the system, anticipating the onset of the jammed phase.
	
	\section{\label{sec:level4} Non-standard percolation of round cells}
	
	\subsection{Susceptibility}
	
	As mentioned above, round cells exhibit a percolation transition. We can see how the largest cluster size vary with density for different values of $N$ in Fig. \ref{fig:round_percolation} (a). To gain insight into the nature of this transition, we analyze the susceptibility $\chi$, defined as the fluctuations of the normalized size of the largest cluster $s_1=\frac{S_1}{N}$:
	
	\begin{equation}
		\chi = \frac{\langle S_1^{2} \rangle - \langle S_1 \rangle^{2}}{N}=N(\langle s_1^{2} \rangle - \langle s_1 \rangle^{2})
	\end{equation}
	
	Fig. \ref{fig:round_percolation} (b) shows the susceptibility for different system sizes $N$. It can be observed that for every size, $\chi$ exhibits a pronounced peak near the critical density $\rho\approx0.86$.

			(c) Finite-Size Scaling of the susceptibility peaks.}

	The scaling of the susceptibility peak maximum provides crucial information about the universality class of the transition. For a finite system of size $L$, the peak of $\chi$ near the critical point behaves as $\chi^{max} \propto L^{\gamma/\nu}$. In our case, we have a box of size $L$ in 2D. Therefore, $N \propto L^{2}$, or $L \propto N^{1/2}$, and then $\chi_{st}^{max} \propto N^{\gamma/2\nu}$. Figure \ref{fig:round_percolation} (c) displays the scaling of the susceptibility peaks with $N$ in a log-log plot. A linear fit yields an effective exponent of $\frac{\gamma}{2\nu}=1.592$.
	

	For standard percolation in 2D, the exponent is $\frac{\gamma}{2\nu} \approx 0.896$. This is very different from the value we obtain. Moreover, for a standard discontinuous transition (first-order), the susceptibility is expected to scale linearly with the system volume ($\chi \propto L^{2}\propto N$). Our observed exponent of $\approx 1.592$ is super-extensive (greater than 1). This implies that the fluctuations grow faster than the system size itself, indicating an anomalously violent regime.
	
	\subsection{Cluster size distributions across the transition}

	To further characterize the nature of this percolation-like transition, we analyze the distribution of cluster sizes. We define the cluster size distribution as
	
	$$
	n_s=\frac{N_s}{N},
	$$
	
	where $N_s$ is the number of clusters of size $s$ and $N$ is the total number of cells. We first study the distribution excluding the largest component, in order to isolate the statistics of finite clusters.
		
	Figure \ref{fig:percolation_round_distribution} displays these distributions for different densities, with the critical density $\rho_c$ (derived from the susceptibility peak) highlighted in every panel. At low densities, as it can be seen in inset (i), the distribution decays rapidly and exhibits a clear cutoff, indicating that round cell clusters remain small and spatially localized.
	
	As the density increases, the distribution progressively broadens and the cutoff shifts toward larger cluster sizes. Interestingly, for intermediate subcritical densities, the distributions already extend over a broad range of sizes, although they still display a clear cutoff.

	Near the critical density, the cluster size distribution does not extend significantly beyond the largest subcritical clusters, but the cutoff becomes much less pronounced and the distribution becomes approximately scale-free. This indicates the coexistence of structures over multiple scales.
	
	Above the transition, finite clusters remain broadly distributed over a similar size range, although their statistical weight decreases as an increasing fraction of the system becomes absorbed into the giant jammed component. For sufficiently high densities ($\rho \gtrapprox0.88$), all round cells belong to the giant cluster and finite clusters become negligible.
	
	
	\begin{figure}[h]
		\centering %agregue esto
		\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/percolation_round/cluster_distribution/cluster_round_distribution_without_giant.eps} %agregue el width
		\caption{\label{fig:percolation_round_distribution} Cluster size distribution without the giant component. Inset (i) shows the regime of densities below $\rho_c$ and (ii) the regime of high densities.}
	\end{figure}

	To quantify the apparent scale-free behavior at the transition, we fit the cluster size distribution at the critical density to a power law,
	
	$$
	n_s \sim s^{-\tau}.
	$$
	
	The distribution is approximately compatible with a power-law decay over an extended intermediate range, yielding an effective exponent
	
	$$
	\tau \approx 0.9,
	$$
	
	for $N=10.000$, as it is shown in figure \ref{fig:tau}.  Although finite-size effects prevent a precise scaling determination, the effective exponent remains close to this value across the explored system sizes.
	
	In usual percolation, the relation $2<\tau<3$ is typically be satisfied. Therefore, our result is in contrast with usual percolation, indicating an anomalous enhancement of large finite clusters near the transition.
	
	\begin{figure}[h]
		\centering %agregue esto
		\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{images/percolation_round/6_normalized_cluster_distribution_fit_N=10000.png} %agregue el width
		\caption{\label{fig:tau} Fit of $\tau$ for $N=10000$.}
	\end{figure}
	
	This picture becomes clearer when the giant component is included in the analysis, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:cluster_size_distribution_giant}. At low densities, the full cluster size distribution remains essentially unchanged, confirming the absence of a dominant connected structure. However, near the critical density, a pronounced peak emerges at very large cluster sizes, revealing the formation of a macroscopic component while finite clusters still persist over a broad range of scales.

	\subsection{Discussion Mechanism for Percolation}
		
	These results show that, although round cells percolate, the underlying mechanism differs from standard percolation. The combination of a continuous growth of the largest cluster followed by an abrupt jump, together with the broad cluster size distributions near the transition, is reminiscent of hybrid phase transitions. In percolation, we have found two different type of percolations that can be associated with our problem.
		
	On one hand, explosive percolation happens when the system avoids the creation of a giant cluster by minimizng the size of the clusters that are being combined. One example is the product rule: two links are chosen and only the one with the minimum result between the product of each cluster is kept. 
	
	On the other hand, bootstrap percolation provides a more natural analogy with the mechanism considered here. In bootstrap percolation, nodes are initially occupied with probability $p$, and are subsequently removed if they have fewer than $k$ occupied neighbors.
	
	An important feature of bootstrap percolation is the possibility of cascades or avalanches, where the removal of one node triggers the subsequent deactivation of many others.
	
	The analogy with our case can be understood by associating occupied nodes in bootstrap percolation with round cells in our system. In that framework, the occupation probability $p$ plays a role analogous to the density $\rho$, since increasing $\rho$ raises the probability of finding crowded regions. The local threshold $k$, which determines whether a node remains occupied, can be related to the amount of available space surrounding each cell.
	
	To further explore this analogy, one relevant quantity is the fraction of active cells, 
	$f_a$, shown in Figure \ref{fig:fraction_active}. As expected, $f_a$ is finite at low densities and decreases as 
	$\rho$ increases. For small system sizes, this decrease appears relatively smooth, whereas for larger systems a jump emerges near the transition. This behavior is consistent with the idea that local changes in cell shape may trigger collective rearrangements involving a large fraction of the system.

	\section{\label{sec:level5} Analysis of elongated clusters and active migration}
		
	In this section, we provide an analysis of the clusters of elongated cells. As previously established, the system can reach two distinct steady states: a jammed state, where all cells remain round and the system is structurally blocked, and an unjammed state, characterized by cell elongation and collective motion. We take into account that averaging across all realizations near the transition can lead to an artificial suppression of the physical observables. For instance, the system polarization may vanish at high densities primarily due to the increasing prevalence of jammed configurations. Therefore, we analyze the system both by considering all realizations and by focusing exclusively on the active branch.

	$$

The results show that the region near $\rho = 0.7$ corresponds to the point of maximum dominance of the largest cluster, indicating the formation of a well-defined oncostream.

	\subsection{Analysis of polarization of elongated cells}

	$$ P = \frac{1}{N_e} \sqrt{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_e} \sin (\varphi_i)\right)^2+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_e} \cos (\varphi_i)\right)^2}, $$

	For the local order parameter, the system is divided into boxes of size comparable to the maximum cell length, and the polarization is computed using the cells within neighboring boxes.
	
	For the global polarization, we observe that when all realizations are considered (panel (a)), the system is ordered at low densities, but the polarization decreases as density increases. At high densities, $P$ increases again, while $\hat{P}$	decays to zero. This behavior is largely due to the presence of jammed configurations. In particular, when only a few cells remain elongated, the normalization in $P$ makes it easier to obtain large values.
	
	When focusing only on the unjammed branch (panel (b)), this effect disappears. In this case, the polarization remains relatively constant ath high densities. Moreover, as the system size increases, the curves tend to flatten, suggesting that for large $N$ the system remains weakly polarized, independently of density.
	
	A similar behavior is observed for the local polarization (panels (c) and (d)). As the system size increases, the local order parameter remains approximately constant but takes high values across the entire active regime (excluding the jammed state).
	
	Therefore, we conclude that, for large system sizes, the system is weakly polarized at the global level but strongly polarized at the local level, across all densities in the unjammed phase. This indicates that density does not significantly affect the degree of alignment of elongated cells, but rather controls whether the system becomes jammed and how clusters are spatially organized.
